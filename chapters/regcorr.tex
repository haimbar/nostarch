\subsection{Association Is Not Causation}

A strong correlation does not show that changing one variable will cause the
other to change. For example, ice cream sales and drowning incidents both tend
to increase in hot weather. Their association does not mean that ice cream
causes drowning; temperature affects both and is a confounding
variable\index{confounding variable}. Reverse causation and selection effects
can create similar problems. Regression can describe an association and make
useful predictions, but neither correlation nor regression alone establishes a
causal relationship\index{causation}.

\section{Regression}
Correlation treats two variables symmetrically: the correlation between $x$ and
$y$ is the same as the correlation between $y$ and $x$. Regression gives the
variables different roles. The outcome we want to explain or predict is the
response variable\index{response variable}, usually denoted by $y$. Variables
used to model the response are called predictors\index{predictor}, explanatory
variables, or covariates\index{covariate}.

Consider body fat percentage. Figure~\ref{fig:underwater} shows an accurate
method that weighs a person underwater.

This procedure, called hydrostatic weighing\index{hydrostatic weighing}, is
expensive, inconvenient, and uncomfortable. Age, weight, and body
circumferences are easier to obtain. Although none determines body fat
perfectly, a model can combine them to estimate body fat percentage.

    objective-methods/hydrostatic-underwater-weighing}{Measurement Toolkit}.}
    \label{fig:underwater}
\end{figure}

We begin with one predictor. The simple linear regression
model\index{simple linear regression} is
\begin{equation*}
  y=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\varepsilon,
\end{equation*}
where $\varepsilon$ is an unobserved error\index{error term} representing
variation not captured by the line. The coefficient $\beta_0$ is the
intercept\index{intercept}: the mean response when $x=0$. The coefficient
$\beta_1$ is the slope\index{slope}: the change in the mean response associated
with a one-unit increase in $x$. The intercept may have no practical meaning if
$x=0$ is outside the observed range. A slope describes association, not
necessarily a causal effect.

The usual simple linear regression assumptions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item The line $\beta_0+\beta_1x$ correctly describes the mean of $y$ at each
value of $x$, so the errors have mean zero.
\item The errors have roughly the same variance for all values of $x$.
\item The errors from different observations are independent.
\item For exact small-sample confidence intervals and tests, the errors are
approximately normally distributed.
\end{itemize}
Normality is not required merely to calculate a least squares line. The first
three assumptions matter most for interpreting the model and its uncertainty.

Let's use the blood pressure data in Figure~\ref{fig:agesbp} to illustrate least
squares\index{least squares}. Estimates are marked with a hat, as in
$\hat\beta_0$ and $\hat\beta_1$.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth,page=3]{images/static/LeastSquares.pdf}
  \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth,page=5]{images/static/LeastSquares.pdf}
  \caption{Idea of least squares}
  \label{fig:fittingLS}
\end{figure}

Any values of $\hat\beta_0$ and $\hat\beta_1$ define a fitted line
$\hat y=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x$. The vertical difference between an observed
response $y_i$ and its fitted value $\hat y_i$ is the residual
$e_i=y_i-\hat y_i$. Unlike the unobserved error $\varepsilon_i$, a residual can
be calculated from the data. Residuals are positive above the line and negative
below it. The shaded squares on the right of Figure~\ref{fig:fittingLS}
represent squared residuals. Least squares chooses the line that minimizes the
residual sum of squares\index{residual sum of squares},
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i - \hat y_i)^2.
\end{equation*}
For simple linear regression, the resulting estimates are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1}
  \hat{\beta}_1 =
  \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}
  {\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2},
  \text{ and }
  \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar y - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar x.
\end{equation}

Equation~(\ref{eq:1}) is tedious to calculate by hand but easy to evaluate in
\R. Under the regression assumptions, standard errors describe how much the
coefficient estimates would vary across repeated samples. They are the basis
for confidence intervals and statistical tests.

\subsection{Outliers and Influential Points}\label{sec:outliers}

What happens if a single data point is way off from the rest? Because the least
squares method squares each vertical distance $(y - \hat{y})^2$, a large
residual contributes disproportionately to the quantity being minimized. In
Figure~\ref{fig:fittingLS}, for example, the patient with a systolic blood
pressure near 220 has a much larger squared residual than the other patients.
Such a point can act like a heavy weight on a seesaw and pull the fitted line
toward itself, although how much it does so also depends on its $x$-value.

Figure~\ref{fig:outliers} illustrates how unusual points can differ:
\begin{itemize}
\item Outliers\index{outlier} in $y$: A point whose $y$-value is far from the
  fitted line and therefore has a large residual. For example, suppose a house
  has 2,000 square feet. Its expected sale price is \$400,000, but it sells for
  \$700,000 instead, making it an outlier in sale price.
\item High-leverage\index{leverage} points in $x$: A point whose $x$-value is
  far from the other $x$-values. For example, a house with 5,000 square feet has
  high leverage in a dataset in which all the other houses range from 1,000 to
  3,000 square feet. If it sells for \$850,000, it follows the overall
  relationship between size and price and is not an outlier in $y$.
\item Influential points\index{influential point}: A point that noticeably
  changes the fitted line when it is included or removed. For example, if the
  house with 5,000 square feet sells for only \$400,000, it pulls the right end
  of the line downward and substantially reduces the estimated slope. Such a
  point is both high-leverage and influential.
\end{itemize}

A common beginner mistake is to immediately delete any unusual point to make
the line fit the rest of the data better. Don't do this! An outlier might be a
simple typo (like entering a height of 700 inches instead of 70 inches), but it
could also be a genuine, exciting discovery or a distinct subpopulation that
reveals how the real world behaves.

The body fat data contain one recorded height of 29.5 inches, which warrants
checking against the original source. We should investigate a suspicious value
before correcting or removing it. In \R, \code{rstandard(fit)} finds large
standardized residuals, \code{hatvalues(fit)} measures leverage, and
\code{cooks.distance(fit)} calculates Cook's distance\index{Cook's distance},
which summarizes how strongly each observation affects the fitted model. These
diagnostics identify observations to investigate, not observations to delete
automatically.


\subsection{How Good Is the Line? Model Diagnostics and
  \texorpdfstring{$R^2$}{R-squared}}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

The \code{lm()} function will fit a line to any dataset, even if the points form
a circle or show only random scatter.

How do we know whether the fitted line is appropriate?

\subsubsection{The $R^2$ Statistic}
A common summary statistic is the coefficient of determination, written
as $R^2$\index{R-squared@$R^2$}. It is the proportion of the variation in the
observed $y$-values that is accounted for by the fitted model.
\begin{itemize}
\item An $R^2 = 1$ (or 100\%) means that every observed $y$-value equals its
  fitted value, so all the points lie on the fitted line.
\item An $R^2 = 0$ means that the fitted model accounts for none of the
  variation in $y$. In simple linear regression with an intercept, the fitted
  line is then horizontal. This does not rule out a nonlinear relationship
  between $x$ and $y$.
\item In simple linear regression with one predictor and an intercept, $R^2$ is
  the square of the sample correlation coefficient $r$. Thus, if $r = 0.8$, then
  $R^2 = 0.64$, meaning that the fitted line accounts for 64\% of the variation
  in $y$.
\end{itemize}

In multiple regression, ordinary $R^2$ never decreases when another predictor
is added, even if that predictor has little value. Adjusted
$R^2$\index{adjusted R-squared@adjusted $R^2$} imposes a penalty for adding
predictors, but neither version measures performance on new data. A test set or
cross-validation is more direct for that purpose.

A high $R^2$ does not guarantee that a straight line has the right shape. A
mild curve can have a high $R^2$ while leaving a pattern in the residuals.
Conversely, Figure~\ref{fig:nonLinear} shows a strong quadratic relationship
whose straight-line $R^2$ is near zero.

\subsubsection{Checking the Residuals}
A residual\index{residual} is the difference between an observed value and its
fitted value, $y - \hat{y}$. A residual plot\index{residual plot} graphs the
residuals against the fitted values and can reveal patterns that $R^2$ misses.
If a straight-line model with constant variance is appropriate, the points
should form a roughly patternless band centered at zero:
\begin{enumerate}
\item No curves: A U-shaped or arched pattern suggests that a straight line has
missed curvature in the relationship.
\item Even spread: The residual spread should remain roughly constant. A fan or
megaphone shape suggests that the variability changes with the fitted value.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:residual-diagnostics} contrasts these patterns. A residual plot
does not directly establish independence, which must be considered from the way
the data were collected. A normal quantile--quantile plot can assess whether
the residual distribution is approximately normal when small-sample inference
depends on that assumption. The command \code{plot(fit)} produces several
standard diagnostic plots in \R.

\subsection{Estimating an Average vs. Predicting a Single Person}
\label{sec:pred_interval}

Suppose we build a regression model to predict student heights from their ages.
For a 15-year-old student, the regression line might give a fitted height of 66
inches. But there are two different questions we could ask:
\begin{enumerate}
\item What is the average height of all 15-year-olds in the population?
\item How tall will one particular 15-year-old be?
\end{enumerate}

Estimating a population average is more precise than predicting the value for
one individual:
\begin{itemize}
\item A confidence interval\index{confidence interval!in regression} gives a
range of plausible values for the population's mean height at a given age. As
the amount of data increases, this interval generally becomes narrower.
\item A prediction interval\index{prediction interval} gives a range of
plausible values for the height of one new student at a given age. It accounts
for both uncertainty in the mean height and natural person-to-person variation.
At the same confidence level, it is therefore wider than the corresponding
confidence interval for the mean.
\end{itemize}

Both intervals are available from \R's \code{predict()} function. For a
confidence interval, use \code{interval = "confidence"}; for a prediction
interval, use \code{interval = "prediction"}.

\subsection{Linear Models Aren't Just Straight Lines}
\label{sec:linear_not_straight}

It is easy to assume that because the method is called ``linear regression'',
it can fit only one straight line relating one predictor to one response. The
body fat model in Equation~(\ref{eq:2}) shows that linear models are more
general.

That model uses five predictors---age, weight, chest circumference, wrist
circumference, and BMI---to estimate body fat percentage. A model with more
than one predictor is called multiple linear
regression\index{multiple linear regression}. The word ``linear'' refers to how
the coefficients enter the model: each predictor is multiplied by a
coefficient, and the resulting terms are added together.

Here BMI is derived from weight and height rather than measured directly. In
this dataset, weight is in pounds and height is in inches. BMI is defined as
\begin{equation*}
  \text{BMI} = 703\frac{\text{Weight}}{\text{Height}^2}.
\end{equation*}
The model is linear in its coefficients and in the columns supplied to
\code{lm()}, including BMI. When written in terms of the raw measurements, it
contains $\text{Weight}/\text{Height}^2$ and is nonlinear in height. Thus, a
linear model can describe a nonlinear relationship\index{nonlinear relationship}
in the raw measurements.

This example also illustrates a potential complication. Weight appears both as
a predictor and inside BMI, so the two predictors contain overlapping
information. Strong overlap among predictors is called
multicollinearity\index{multicollinearity}. It can make individual coefficient
estimates unstable even when the model predicts reasonably well. This is one
reason to interpret coefficients in a multiple regression cautiously.

\subsection{Don't Step off the Cliff: The Danger of Extrapolation}
\label{sec:extrapolation}

Imagine tracking a baby's growth during the first year of life. Suppose the baby
is 20 inches long at birth and grows 10 inches by age 1. If we extend that rate
to age 25, the predicted height is 270 inches, or 22.5 feet.

This use of a model outside the observed range of $x$-values is called
extrapolation\index{extrapolation}. The farther we move beyond the data, the
less reason we have to expect the fitted pattern to continue.

By contrast, interpolation\index{interpolation} means predicting within the
observed range. A straight line might approximate the relationship well within
that range even when the pattern changes beyond it. Growth may slow or stop, a
trend may reverse or reach a physical boundary, and practical constraints such
as fuel, battery capacity, or daylight may begin to matter.

Plugging an extreme $x$-value into a formula is easy, but the result may be
meaningless or physically impossible. Always ask: \emph{``Is this prediction
within the range of the data we actually collected?''}

\section{Summary}

This chapter introduced two complementary tools for studying relationships.
Scatterplots reveal the form, direction, and unusual features of an
association, while correlation summarizes the direction and strength of a
linear association. Correlation does not establish causation. Regression goes
further by distinguishing a response from its predictors and describing how
the mean response changes as the predictors change.

We used least squares to fit linear models and residual plots to check their
assumptions and identify unusual observations. We also examined $R^2$, model
validation, confidence and prediction intervals, multiple and categorical
predictors, and interactions. These tools make linear models flexible, but no
summary or fitted equation replaces careful diagnostics. Predictions are most
trustworthy for populations and predictor ranges represented by the data, so
extrapolation requires particular caution.
