\chapter{Estimation: A Hide-and-Seek Game}
\label{ch:estimation}

Imagine trying to guess the average height of all 12-year-old boys in the
United States. You could measure a few hundred children, but you could
never measure them all. Yet governments, scientists, businesses, and sports
analysts make this kind of guess every day. They use limited data to learn
about quantities that are hidden from view, such as averages, percentages,
or relationships between variables. This challenge is the heart of
estimation. It's a hide-and-seek game that we play with nature: the
answers we want are hidden, and the sample provides clues about where
they might be. In the previous chapter, we focused on deciding whether
an observed effect could be explained by chance alone. Now we turn to a
different question: if a quantity exists, how can we estimate its value
and how confident should we be in our estimate? In this chapter, we'll
learn how to construct estimators and judge their quality using bias
and mean squared error, use maximum likelihood to pick good estimates,
build confidence intervals to quantify our uncertainty, and use the
bootstrap to approximate that uncertainty when large-sample theory
falls short.


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Let's set up a simple hide-and-seek game in a concrete setting. 
Suppose a population is modeled by a normal distribution with an 
unknown location parameter~$\mu$, which we may think of as the true 
average height of all 12-year-old boys in the United States. To keep the 
focus on the core ideas of estimation rather than algebraic details, we 
work with a simplified version of this model and assume the population 
variance~$\sigma^2$
is known; without loss of generality, we set $\sigma^2 = 1$. We 
observe a random sample of size $n$ and use it to learn about the hidden 
value of~$\mu$.

An estimator of $\mu$ is required to be computable from the observed data and
shouldn't depend on any unknown quantities. Any number that can be computed
solely from the sample is called a \emph{statistic}. \index{statistic}
When a statistic is used to estimate a population quantity, it's called an
\emph{estimator}. \index{estimator} Common examples include the 
\emph{mean} (the average of the observed values), the \emph{median} 
(the middle value when the observed data is sorted), the \emph{range}
(the difference between the largest and smallest observations), and the 
\emph{midrange} (the average of the maximum and minimum values in the sample).  
With these tools in place, several natural questions arise:
\begin{enumerate}
\item What properties are desirable for an estimator?
\item When multiple estimators are available for the same target, how do we 
      assess which one is better?
\item How do we assess the uncertainty of an estimator?
\end{enumerate}


\section{Point Estimation: Seeking Strategies}

For a normal distribution, the location parameter $\mu$ equals the mean and
median. Two natural ways to estimate the center are the sample mean and the
sample median. A third possibility is the midrange, the average of the smallest
and largest observations. Each estimator uses different parts of the data,
so they can behave differently in practice. 

When an estimator is computed for a given sample, its value is called an
estimate\index{estimate}. That is, an estimator is a strategy and an estimate is
its realization given a dataset. (Note that the term \emph{point estimate} is
often used to emphasize that the estimate is a single number, as opposed to a
range of plausible values.)

Which of the three estimators is the best? In this game, Nature knows the true

When we compare different methods of estimating $\mu$, the comparison only
applies to the specific data sample we're looking at. A method that is usually
good might give worse results than a ``bad'' method just by chance for that
specific sample.  To assess the quality of an estimator, we can investigate its
properties; after all, an estimator is just a formula or rule that uses a random
sample to estimate something. Because the estimator depends on random data, it's
also random. Here are some properties about estimators:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If the average result of an estimator (over many random samples) is equal
  to the true value we're trying to estimate, then this estimator is
  \emph{unbiased}\index{estimator!unbiased}.  Mathematically, this is often
  written as $\operatorname{E}(\hat\mu) = \mu$, where
  $\operatorname{E}(\cdot)$ is the mean operator, which returns the
  mean of the random quantities in the parentheses. Here
  $\operatorname{E}(\hat\mu)$ gives the mean of $\hat\mu$ over all
  possible random samples.
\item The \emph{mean squared error (MSE)}\index{mean squared error} measures how
  far, on average, the estimator's result is from the true target in
  squared distance. It combines
  both the error (or bias) and how spread out the estimates are. The formula is
  $\operatorname{E}[(\hat\mu - \mu)^2]$.
  An estimator with a smaller MSE than another estimator is more
  \emph{efficient}\index{estimator!efficient} than the other one, because it
  is closer to the truth in squared distance on average.
\end{enumerate}

Most of the theoretical properties of an estimator are studied asymptotically,
that is, assuming that the sample size goes to infinity. For finite
sample sizes, their properties are easily investigated via
hide-and-seek games, or simulations. In
each simulation setting, we set up the hide-and-seek game and play the game
repeatedly for a large number of replicates. The bias and the MSE of an
estimator can be approximated by the sample averages from the
simulated replicates.

\subsection{Maximum Likelihood Estimator}
To play the hide-and-seek game better, pick a strategy that makes the
observed data plausible. The idea of maximum likelihood estimation is
simple: choose the parameter value that makes the observed data most
likely. Which strategy is best depends on how the data was generated,
which we usually don't know, but maximum likelihood often produces
highly efficient estimates under broad conditions.


Let's start with a simple example.
\begin{example}[Likelihood in guessing jars]
\label{ex:has-jars}
There are nine jars. The first jar has nine red balls and one green ball; the
second jar has eight red balls and two green balls; $\cdots\cdots$; the ninth
jar has one red ball and nine green balls. A jar is selected uniformly at
random, and then one ball
is drawn uniformly at random from that jar. Suppose the ball drawn is red.
Which jar is the most likely to have been selected?


Figure~\ref{fig:ch6:jars} shows the nine jars. Each jar is a candidate
explanation for the red ball we observed: it tells us the probability of
drawing red if that jar were the one selected. In other words, the nine jars
are the nine possible likelihoods, and our job is to find the jar (the
probability) under which the observed color is most likely.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{images/static/jars.pdf}
\caption{The nine jars, each with a different mix of red and green balls;
red squares represent red balls and green squares represent green balls.}
\label{fig:ch6:jars}
\end{figure}

There are nine possibilities. If the selected jar were the first one, the
probability that a randomly selected ball is red is 9/10. If the selected jar
were the second one, the probability that a randomly selected ball is red is
8/10. Continue until the ninth jar. With this calculation, which jar should we
guess?


The first one!
\end{example}


The principle that we use here is the so-called \emph{maximum likelihood
estimation}\index{maximum likelihood estimation}. The \emph{parameter
space}\index{parameter space}, that is, the collection of all possible
values of the parameter, consists of only nine jars. We choose the one that
gives the highest likelihood of observing a randomly selected ball being red.


For problems with continuous parameter spaces, the principle remains the
same. We compute the joint density of the observed sample as a function of the
parameters, and call this function the \emph{likelihood}\index{likelihood}
function. Then we maximize the likelihood with respect to the parameters. The
resulting maximizer is the \emph{maximum likelihood estimator (MLE)} of the
parameters. In R, a convenient function for finding MLE for univariate
distributions is \lstinline{fitdistr} in package \lstinline{MASS}.


\begin{example}[Maximum likelihood estimator]
\label{ex:mle}
\runR{Code/est-mle-binom.R}{mle-binom}
Suppose a basketball player takes $20$ free throws during practice and
makes $16$ of them. We would like to estimate the player's true free-throw
success probability $p$.

One possible guess is $p = 0.50$, meaning that the player makes about half
of all free throws. Another possibility is $p = 0.80$. Which value is more
believable given the observed data?

The idea of maximum likelihood estimation is simple: choose the parameter
value that makes the observed data most likely. For a binomial model, the
likelihood of observing $16$ successful shots out of $20$ attempts is
\[
  L(p) = \binom{20}{16} p^{16}(1-p)^4 .
\]

We can evaluate this expression for many possible values of $p$ between
$0$ and $1$ and see which one gives the largest likelihood. Because
the log transformation is monotonically increasing, the solution is the
same when we maximize the log-likelihood instead:
\[
  \log L(p) = \log\binom{20}{16} + 16\log(p) + 4\log(1-p).
\]
\showChunk{R}{Code/est-mle-binom.R}{loglik}

Finally, we plot the log-likelihood curve and mark the
maximum-likelihood estimate with a dashed vertical line.
\showChunk{R}{Code/est-mle-binom.R}{plotloglik}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch6:loglik-binom} shows the log-likelihood as a function of
$p$ for the observed data (16 successes out of 20). The dashed vertical line
marks the maximum-likelihood estimate.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{images/chapter_6/loglik_binom.pdf}
\caption{Log-likelihood for the binomial example (16/20), with the
maximum-likelihood estimate at $\hat p = 0.80$ marked by a dashed line}
\label{fig:ch6:loglik-binom}
\end{figure}

% (Hint: compare likelihood values at a few candidate p's.)
% Answer: The likelihood is highest near $p=0.80$, which is the
% observed proportion of successes.

The largest likelihood occurs when $p$ is near
\inlnR{```cat(phat[1])```}[phat]. In fact, for this problem, the
MLE is exactly $\hat p = 16/20 = 0.80$.


This result matches our intuition. A player who made $16$ out of $20$ shots
is much more likely to have a true success probability near $80\%$ than near
$50\%$. This example illustrates a central idea in estimation: good parameter
estimates are values that make the observed data look plausible.
\end{example}

In more advanced texts, you'll learn that the MLE is often the most
efficient estimator under fairly general regularity conditions. However,
this also means that when these regularity conditions are violated, the
MLE may no longer be the most efficient estimator.

\section{Uncertainty}
So far, every estimate we've computed---the sample mean, the MLE, and
so on---has been a single number called a \emph{point
estimate}\index{point estimate}: our best single guess at the unknown
parameter. A point estimate on its own doesn't say how far off it
might be, so to answer how sure we should be about an estimate, we
build a range of plausible values instead. This range is called a
confidence interval. A simple way to think about a confidence interval
is: it's an interval, computed from the sample, that is likely to
contain the unknown target value. A confidence interval with level
$1-\alpha$ aims to capture the true parameter in about
$100(1-\alpha)\%$ of all possible samples.

\subsection{Large Sample Approximation}
One way to construct confidence intervals is to lean on the large-sample
guarantees from Chapter~\ref{ch:llnclt}. The law of large numbers tells
us that as the sample size grows, the sample mean gets closer and
closer to the true population mean~$\mu$---this is why the sample mean
is a sensible point estimate in the first place. The central limit
theorem goes further: it describes the shape of the sample mean's own
distribution around~$\mu$ for large samples. In practice we only ever
collect one sample, not many, so we never actually see that
distribution directly---but knowing its shape is exactly what lets us
build an interval around the single sample mean we do observe and
attach a confidence level to it.

In the simple situation of estimating the population mean of a
distribution, for example, we can use the sample mean and the central
limit theorem to construct an interval centered at the sample mean.
Suppose that we have a sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ based on a sample of
size~$n$ from a population with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. As
we just discussed, $\bar{X}_n$'s behavior can be approximated by a
normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2/n$, if the
sample size $n$ is large. Using this fact, we can construct the
following interval
\[
\left(\bar{X}_n - z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \quad
\bar{X}_n + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right),
\]
where $z_{\alpha/2}$ is the number so that the area between
$-z_{\alpha/2}$ and $z_{\alpha/2}$ under the standard normal density
curve is $100(1-\alpha)\%$. In R, this quantile is computed with
\lstinline{qnorm(1 - alpha / 2)}; for a 95\% interval ($\alpha = 0.05$),
this gives the familiar $z_{\alpha/2} \approx 1.96$. This is a confidence
interval for $\mu$ whose confidence level is asymptotically $1 - \alpha$.
A 95\% confidence interval means $\alpha = 0.05$.


When the population variance $\sigma^2$ is unknown, we can use the sample
variance $S_n^2$ in its place. For a finite sample, this substitution is
more precise if we also replace $z_{\alpha/2}$ with $t_{n-1,\alpha/2}$, the
corresponding quantile of the \emph{$t$-distribution}\index{$t$-distribution}
with $n-1$ degrees of freedom, whose heavier tails account for the extra
uncertainty introduced by estimating $\sigma$ from the data rather than
knowing it. As $n$ grows, $t_{n-1,\alpha/2}$ converges to $z_{\alpha/2}$, so
using $z_{\alpha/2}$ with $S_n$ in place of $\sigma$---the approach we take
in the rest of this section---still gives an interval that is asymptotically
valid, even though it's not exact for small $n$.


The \emph{coverage probability}\index{coverage probability} of a confidence
interval \emph{procedure} is the long-run fraction of intervals, built the
same way from repeated samples, that actually contain the true parameter.
Imagine drawing a new sample of size $n$, computing a new interval from it,
and repeating this many times: the coverage probability is the fraction of
those intervals that capture the true value. A confidence interval with
confidence level~$1-\alpha$ is constructed so that, ideally, its coverage
probability equals~$1-\alpha$. In practice this may not hold exactly,
especially when the interval relies on an asymptotic approximation---like
the CLT-based interval above---that has not yet kicked in at the sample
size we have.


It's worth being precise about what coverage probability is not. Once a
particular interval has been computed from one particular sample, that
interval either does or doesn't contain the true parameter---there's no
probability left to speak of. The $100(1-\alpha)\%$ describes the
procedure, evaluated over hypothetical repeated samples, not our certainty
about this one interval. This is the same distinction we drew for p-values
in Chapter~\ref{ch:hypothesis}: a p-value describes how surprising the data
is under $H_0$, not the probability that $H_0$ is true; a confidence
interval describes how often the procedure succeeds, not the chance that
this particular interval succeeded.

distribution}\index{gamma distribution}, so it helps to have a quick
picture of it first. It's a continuous distribution for positive-valued
data---think waiting times, amounts, or other quantities that can't go
below zero and tend to have a long right tail. It's controlled by a
\emph{shape} parameter and a \emph{scale} parameter, and its mean is
simply their product: shape~$\times$~scale. A small shape value gives a
sharp peak near zero with a long tail; a large shape value makes the
distribution look more symmetric and bell-shaped. R's \lstinline{rgamma()}
function generates gamma-distributed data from a given shape and scale.

\subsection{Connection to Hypothesis Testing}
Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests answer the same underlying
question from two directions, and either one can be built from the other.
Consider testing $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ against $H_1: \mu \ne \mu_0$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:hypothesis}) with the test statistic
\[
  T = \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu_0}{S_n / \sqrt{n}}.
\]
At significance level $\alpha$, this test rejects $H_0$ exactly when
$|T| > z_{\alpha/2}$---which happens exactly when $\mu_0$ falls outside the
interval $\bar{X}_n \pm z_{\alpha/2} S_n/\sqrt{n}$, the very confidence
interval constructed above. So a hypothesized value $\mu_0$ is rejected by
the level-$\alpha$ test if and only if it lies outside the
$100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval; equivalently, the confidence
interval is exactly the set of null values $\mu_0$ that the test would
\emph{fail} to reject. This is why reporting a confidence interval is often
more informative than reporting a single test's outcome: instead of
answering one yes-or-no question about one $\mu_0$, the interval answers
that question for every possible $\mu_0$ at once.

Constructing confidence intervals depends on the uncertainty of the
estimator. Often, a point estimator can be easily obtained, for example,
through optimizing an objective function or solving some estimating
equations. The variation of a point estimator, however, can be much more
difficult to obtain.


\emph{Bootstrap}\index{bootstrap}
is a powerful tool to approximate the uncertainty of an estimator.
As a self-assisted approach, it approximates the variation of the estimator by
treating the sample as a population and resampling repeatedly from it with
replacement, each time forming a so-called bootstrap sample of the same size as
the original sample. For each bootstrap sample, we can obtain the point
estimator. The variation of the original point estimator can be approximated by
the empirical variation of a collection of the point estimators obtained from
the bootstrap samples.



Here we demonstrate how well the bootstrap can approximate the variance of some
simple statistics. Suppose that \lstinline{statistic} is a function that takes a
random sample \lstinline{x} as input and returns a scalar statistic. A bootstrap
procedure to obtain a large number \lstinline{R} of bootstrap copies of the
statistic from a sample \lstinline{x} is implemented in the following function
\lstinline{myboot}.
\showChunk{R}{Code/est-ci-boot.R}{myboot}


The function \lstinline{myboot} returns a list with two components. The first
component \lstinline{t0} is the scalar statistic computed from the observed
sample \lstinline{x}; the second component \lstinline{t} is a vector
of dimension \lstinline{R}, each element of which is a scalar
statistic computed from a
bootstrap sample. We use the empirical variation of \lstinline{t} to approximate
the variation of \lstinline{t0}. A 95\% confidence interval can be constructed
simply by the 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles of \lstinline{t}. This type of confidence
interval is known as \emph{percentile bootstrap confidence
interval}\index{bootstrap!percentile confidence interval}.

\section{Summary}

In this chapter we studied how data can be used to estimate unknown
features of a population. Through the hide-and-seek games, we saw that
different estimators---the sample mean, median, and midrange---can
behave very differently depending on the underlying distribution, and
we used bias and mean squared error, estimated from many simulated
replicates, to compare them. We also introduced maximum likelihood
estimation, which selects parameter values that make the observed data
most plausible. Because a single point estimate doesn't convey how
confident we should be, we studied confidence intervals built from
large-sample theory and examined their coverage probabilities through
simulation.
Finally, we introduced the bootstrap, a computational method that
approximates the variability of an estimator by resampling from the
observed data, which is useful when the large-sample approximation is
not reliable. Everything in this chapter assumed that our sample was
trustworthy to begin with. In the next chapter, we turn to data
collection itself: what makes a sample representative of the
population we care about, and what can go wrong when it isn't.
